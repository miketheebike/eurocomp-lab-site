Why and When

Program design first, method second. This page covers why evaluation is worth doing, when it has to be decided, and how to choose between the available designs.

Two purposes

Impact evaluation has two purposes: policy learning and accountability

Learning through impact evaluation is a continuous process

Every evaluation raises new questions along with the ones it answers, each round building on the last. That is how we expand our knowledge of what works, for whom, and under what conditions, and use it to design better programs. Exchange within a community of practice matters, so we can learn from each other, not just from our own results.

Review Review a programme’s theory of change, identify risks and learning opportunities. Review lessons from other settings and countries, adapt them, test them in a new context. Identify baseline needs of potential beneficiaries in your region.

Test Test different programme designs and delivery modes to identify what works best. Assess for whom the programme works best. Address take-up challenges, both over and undersubscription. Learn about mechanisms, unintended effects, and crowding out.

Invest Get a quantitative and credible impact estimate that can guide future investments. Conduct cost-benefit analyses to avoid wasting money and to inform programme decisions. Scale up based on evidence.

Is it relevant for you

Think impact evaluation is not relevant for you?

Whether you have sufficient evidence that your programme works, or you are still working out who it reaches and how to design it, there is a case for evaluation. The starting question is whether the programme is working as well as it could be.

You want to learn who to target and how to design it

Too many applicants? Consider introducing randomization in the middle ranks.

Too few applicants? Consider simplifying access and using an encouragement design.

You know it is effective, but is it efficient?

Test how you deliver it. Compare different ways of delivering the programme to the same beneficiaries, testing changes that improve efficiency and satisfaction.

An impact evaluation is not useful only if you already have enough evidence on returns for your target group and region, and there is no interest in learning about different ways of implementing the programme for the same beneficiaries.

M&E versus impact evaluation

What is the difference between monitoring and evaluation and impact evaluation?

Monitoring

Collect data on inputs, outputs, outcomes and participants to:

  • Improve day-to-day management and decisions
  • Make descriptive before and after comparisons

Describes what is happening, but not whether it is because of the intervention.

Impact evaluation

Collect data on participants and control groups to:

  • Assess the difference between participating and not
  • Establish causality
  • Support cost-benefit analysis

Determines impact in a way that can only be attributed to the programme, by establishing what would have happened without it.

Allocating public resources

What does good allocation of public resources look like?

Not always what you would expect at first glance. Impact evaluation can help improve allocation.

Strategy A: fund the top performers

Firms that succeed: 80 of 100

Would have succeeded anyway: 65

Caused by the grant: 15

Looks stronger: 80 successes.

Strategy B: fund the marginal firm

Firms that succeed: 55 of 100

Would have succeeded anyway: 20

Caused by the grant: 35

Actually causes more impact: 35 caused.

Deadweight: low additionality

Top performers would likely have invested anyway. Public funds substitute for private money they would have spent regardless, generating little truly additional activity per euro.

Selection bias in measurement

Comparing funded, stronger firms with unfunded, weaker ones credits the programme with firms’ pre-existing quality, not with what the grant did.

Where the grant buys most

Support actually changes behaviour for the marginal, constrained firm: the good project that could not otherwise get funded.

The counterfactual

What exactly is the impact of a programme?

Definition. Impact is what happens after a programme is introduced, minus what would have happened in its absence, the counterfactual.

The dilemma. The counterfactual is a “what if” and is unknown to us.

The solution. Find a way to construct the counterfactual so impact can be measured. This can be done in different ways. The methods below show why randomized controlled trials are considered the gold standard.

Finding the counterfactual

Strategy. Find a group of firms that on average look like treated firms would have looked in the absence of the programme. This group is the comparison group.

Randomized controlled trials

Create the comparison group up front, by randomly assigning who receives the programme. Randomization is what guarantees the two groups look the same on average.

Ex-post and quasi-experimental designs

Look for a suitable comparison group after the programme has already been rolled out, using natural variation in who received it and who did not.

Selection bias is the risk that the comparison group does not actually look like the treatment group on average. If that assumption is wrong, the impact estimate is biased.

Randomized controlled trials

What is a randomized controlled trial?

Intuition. Randomly assign the programme so one group receives it (the treatment group) and one does not (the control or comparison group). Because the two groups start out the same, the control group’s outcome shows what would have happened to the treatment group without the programme.

Advantage. RCTs are considered the gold standard of quantitative impact evaluation because:

  • No assumptions about outcome levels or trends between groups are necessary, if randomized within groups
  • They deliver strong evidence
  • They are easy to interpret

Randomization can improve legitimacy and trust in programmes

Randomly selecting beneficiaries among eligible applicants is fair. When more eligible firms apply than there is budget to fund, a lottery is a transparent way to decide who gets in, treating every eligible applicant the same rather than relying on favouritism or timing.

What “random” means here. Not randomly sampling a sub-population to study, but randomly deciding, among firms that already qualify, who receives the programme and who does not.

When you cannot randomize

What if you cannot randomize?

An RCT only works if randomization is built in from day one, integrated at design stage before the programme launches, not added afterward. When that is not possible, quasi-experimental methods are the alternative.

Quasi-experimental methods always rely on assumptions, some stronger than others. If those assumptions do not hold, the impact estimate is biased. Many of these assumptions are not testable. The credibility of the evaluation depends on the credibility of the assumptions made.

Pre-post (before and after)

Method
Compare programme participants before and after the programme.
Identifying assumption
In the absence of the programme, participants would have had the same measured outcomes as before. That means no other factors or events, for example economic shocks, affect the outcome besides the programme. This is a strong assumption that rarely holds in practice.

For example, sales might rise after a grant simply because the whole regional economy is recovering at the same time, not because of the programme.

Simple difference

Method
Compare programme participants with non-participants.
Identifying assumption
There is no difference between participants and non-participants besides programme participation. The reason for participation must be unrelated to outcomes. Again, this is unlikely to hold in practice.

For example, firms that apply for a grant are often more ambitious or better managed than those that do not, so they might grow faster regardless of the grant.

Difference in differences

Method
Find a group with the same trend and compare differences over time.
Identifying assumption
Any other factors affecting participants over time are the same for non-participants, so both groups would follow the same trajectory in the absence of treatment. This is the parallel trends assumption, and it cannot be tested.

For example, if grant recipients are concentrated in areas hit harder by a regional downturn than the comparison firms, their trends could diverge even without the programme.

Matching

Method
Participants are matched with non-participants who share the same observable characteristics. Matching tries to mimic an RCT. It can be done in different ways: a participant can be matched with one non-participant, or with a weighted average of all similar non-participants, where closer matches count more.
Identifying assumption
There are no differences between participants and matched non-participants in unobserved characteristics that affect the outcome.

For example, two firms matched on size and sector might still differ in the owner’s ambition or management quality, both of which would likely affect outcomes.

Regression discontinuity design

Method
Compare participants who were just eligible with those who were just ineligible. This does not measure impact for all participants, only for those at the cut-off, which is why the result is called a local average treatment effect. The advantage is that unobservable characteristics stop being a concern.
Identifying assumption
The programme is administered following pre-defined cut-off rules, the criteria determining who falls above or below are not manipulated, a large number of units sit around the cut-off, and nothing else changes suddenly at the cut-off.

For example, if firms know grants are awarded below a certain revenue threshold, some might understate revenue slightly to stay just below it, so firms just below the cut-off are no longer comparable to those just above.

When not to evaluate

Reasons not to measure impact via policy experimentation

01

Not the right tool

The question is excellent but is not about impact, for example satisfaction with services or cost per service. Monitoring and feedback data are easier and do not need a counterfactual.

02

Not yet

The theory of change is not clear, or implementation is unlikely to work because the markets to serve do not exist.

03

Statistically not feasible

Too few units or too little money. Large indirect or spillover effects you cannot measure. Setting too chaotic, for example disaster relief. Treatment assigned at too high a level, for example national policy.

04

Maybe not worth it for learning

The answer is already known from credible existing studies, or there is no generalizable “why” because the programme is one-off with little scale or replication potential.

Risks

I have excess demand and a well designed RCT. What can go wrong?

01

Non-compliance and take-up

Not every firm assigned to a programme ends up participating. Comparing everyone as originally assigned (the intention-to-treat effect) stays unbiased, since assignment was random, but it understates the effect on those who actually participated.

02

Attrition

Participants may drop out before outcomes can be measured, or stop responding to follow-up data collection. The risk is that dropout is not random: if struggling firms are the ones most likely to disappear, the firms you do measure make the programme look better than it is.

03

Spillovers and contamination

The comparison group is not always as isolated as it looks. Know-how from a business training programme can spread by word of mouth to non-participating competitors or suppliers, so the comparison group partly benefits too, understating the true effect.
