The running example: SMART Romania

EU-funded grants of EUR 15,000 to 100,000 for digital tools, equipment, and services.

Randomization has to be designed into your programme from the start

An RCT is not something that can be added to a programme after launch. The comparison group, the eligibility rules, and the baseline data all have to be settled before the first grant is awarded.

How many firms do I need?

Once you know who is eligible, the next question is whether enough firms are available to measure an impact. Three things determine the answer.

Expected impact

The smaller the effect you expect, the more firms you need to detect it.

Desired precision

More certainty about the answer requires more observations.

Budget and capacity

Every firm in the study needs support, monitoring, and surveys.

Pool too small? Loosen the eligibility bar, or widen the ranking bins.

How the lottery worked

With 1,038 firms in the pool and grants for only some, SMART Romania needed a fair way to choose.

In SMART Romania, the draw, and nothing else, decided which firms received support.

To confirm Exact treatment and control split, and whether the draw was public or computerized.

Public draw or private draw?

Public lottery

A public draw, for example pulling names from a hat.

Advantage: transparent and fair.

Disadvantage: the comparison group may change its behaviour once it knows the result.

Private lottery

A lottery run behind the scenes.

Advantage: avoids the comparison group reacting to a public result.

Disadvantage: less visibly fair.

In practice, many World Bank-supported evaluations run the draw as a secure, documented, computerized procedure that can be re-checked afterwards.

Narrowing the pool: a lottery around a cutoff

Score firms on eligibility criteria. Accept the top scorers outright, reject the bottom outright, and randomize only the firms in between.

Best when: eligibility criteria are clear and the programme is oversubscribed, with limited resources.

Keep selection rules-based. Subjective rankings, even by judges or expert panels, have been shown to introduce hidden bias that a lottery or scored cutoff avoids.

Why a lottery?

Fair

In SMART Romania, every eligible firm had the same chance of receiving a grant. Selection could not be influenced by connections or favouritism.

Defensible

Rules reduce bias. Across several studies, even expert judges struggle to predict which firms will succeed.

Efficient

One online survey serves as application, eligibility check, and baseline data collection.

What happens after the lottery?

The evaluation now steps back and lets the programme do its work.

Treatment firms

Receive the digitalization grants and put them to work: equipment, software, services.

Comparison firms

Continue business as usual, without a grant.

Same markets, same conditions, same surveys. The only difference between SMART Romania’s groups is the grant.

Why randomize?

Now that both groups have been followed over time, the final question is how to attribute any differences to the programme.

“What would have happened without the programme?” Randomization makes it possible to answer this question.

Firms drawn in the lottery

Received digitalization grants.

Firms not drawn

No grants; surveyed identically.

Because the lottery created two comparable groups, the difference between them is the true impact of the programme.

Five practical ways to randomize your programme

SMART Romania used one approach: the lottery. Many programmes need a different design.

Lottery

How: randomly select who gets the programme, by public or private lottery.

Best when: eligibility is clear and the programme is oversubscribed.

Multiple treatment arms

How: randomly assign different versions of the programme, with or without a control group.

Best when: you want to know which design works best, not just whether it works.

Randomized phase-in

How: everyone gets the programme eventually, in a random order. Those still waiting are the comparison group.

Best when: budget or capacity cannot cover everyone at once.

Randomized intensities

How: vary how much of the programme a firm gets, for example 100%, 50%, or 0% of eligible employees.

Best when: spillovers within or between firms are a concern.

Encouragement design

How: randomize who gets extra outreach to take up the programme, since it cannot be denied to anyone.

Best when: take-up is voluntary and the programme itself cannot be withheld.

Multiple treatment arms

Randomly assign firms to different versions of the programme, plus a comparison group.

Best when: you want to know which version works best, not just whether the programme works. Trade-off: the sample is split across more groups, so you need more firms.

SMART Romania✓ One treatment group versus one comparison group

Randomized phase-in

Everyone receives the programme eventually, in a random order. Firms still waiting form the comparison group.

Best when: budget or capacity cannot cover everyone at once. Trade-off: long-term impacts cannot be observed.

SMART Romania✓ Not used: a one-time lottery with a lasting comparison group

Randomized intensities

Randomly vary how much of the programme each firm gets, for example 100%, 50%, or none of eligible employees.

Best when: spillovers are likely, for example information spreading between treated and untreated employees. Trade-off: detecting effects at each level needs a bigger sample.

SMART Romania✓ Not used: one level of support was compared with none

Encouragement design

Low take-up is common even for free programmes. Randomize who gets extra encouragement, then compare the whole encouraged group with the whole not-encouraged group.

Compliance is never perfect. Some encouraged firms never take the programme up, and some others do anyway. Compare the full groups regardless.
