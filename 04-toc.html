Step 1: Define a Theory of Change (ToC) for your IE

What is a Theory of Change?

A Theory of Change is a causal chain between inputs, activities, outputs, outcomes, and goals of an instrument or policy. It provides a clear understanding of the mechanisms through which policy makers intend an instrument or policy to generate desired outcomes or long-term goals.

Components of a Theory of Change

The ToC follows a linear causal chain, with assumptions sitting between each step:

Inputs

Resources, efforts, and actions invested in a policy — financial resources, personnel, equipment, training programs, etc.

Activities

Actions and tasks undertaken using the inputs to deliver the intended outputs. The concrete work of the policy or instrument — e.g., running grant calls, delivering training, disbursing subsidies, providing advisory services.

Outputs

Direct and immediate results of the activities conducted within a policy. Tangible and observable products or deliverables that demonstrate the completion of specific tasks.

Short-Term Outcomes

Immediate changes that occur as a direct result of the outputs. Often related to changes in knowledge, attitudes, skills, and behaviors among the target population. They play a crucial role in achieving the larger objectives.

Medium-Term Outcomes

Intermediate changes that occur due to sustained efforts and progress from short-term outcomes. Indicate progress toward the overall goal and often involve shifts in social norms, policies, or organizational practices.

Goals

The policy’s broad, long-term aspirations or objectives. Describe the ultimate impact or change that the policy aims to achieve. Goals are usually overarching and may require significant time and effort to accomplish.

Assumptions

Assumptions are the conditions that must hold for each link in the causal chain to lead to the next. They sit between every step and represent the risks to the logic. An impact evaluation exists to test these assumptions; making them explicit tells you what to measure and where the logic is most likely to break.

Why Invest Time in Developing a ToC?

1. It sharpens the design of your evaluation - Generates clear research questions and defines which outcomes to measure and what data to collect - Can be built participatorily by starting from the final outcomes and working backwards - Prompts a credibility check: is there evidence to support each step in the causal chain?

2. It produces richer, more transferable policy lessons - Measuring the right intermediate outcomes reveals how and why an instrument works, not just whether it works - Opening this “black box” turns results into actionable insight rather than a single verdict - The resulting knowledge generalises better to other contexts, settings, and instruments

Defining Evaluation Questions

Defining clear evaluation questions is the starting point of any rigorous impact evaluation. A well-formulated evaluation question specifies what the intervention is, who it targets, what outcome is expected to change, and over what time horizon. Questions should be grounded in the Theory of Change — each one should correspond to a link in the causal chain that the evaluation seeks to test. In practice, it helps to distinguish between the primary evaluation question, which typically concerns the overall effect of the intervention on a key outcome, and secondary questions, which probe mechanisms, heterogeneous effects across subgroups, or the role of specific assumptions. Evaluation questions should also be realistic: they must be answerable with the data and methods available, and scoped to what the evaluation can credibly attribute to the intervention rather than to external factors.

Step 2: Build Relevant Indicators

From ToC to Indicators

A ToC plays a vital role as a foundation for developing relevant indicators to assess the impacts of a policy. The elements of a ToC reflect constructs that need further definition to be measurable. Indicators should be defined for each pillar of the ToC (inputs, activities, outputs, outcomes, goals, and assumptions) to enable the measurement and verification of change.

Well-Defined Indicators (SMART)

Criterion Description
Specific To measure the information required as closely as possible
Measurable To ensure that the information can be readily obtained (usually in numerical form)
Attributable To ensure that each measure is linked to the project’s efforts
Realistic To ensure that the data can be obtained in a timely fashion, with reasonable frequency, and at a reasonable cost
Targeted To the objective population

From Indicators to Data

Indicators rarely need to be built from scratch. Where possible, practitioners should draw on existing, well-tested measures from similar studies, evidence gap maps, and validated frameworks such as the CIS or WB Enterprise Surveys. Once indicators are defined, each one requires a concrete data source to back it up. Step 3 addresses this by answering, for every indicator, what needs to be collected, what type of data is appropriate (primary or secondary), how it should be gathered, from whom, and at what point in time. Crucially, these decisions must be made early — before randomization begins — to ensure the evaluation is properly set up to measure what it intends to.

Step 3: Collect Appropriate Data

First-Order Decisions

Before any data collection begins, five fundamental questions need to be answered:

  1. What do you need to measure?
  2. What type of data is needed to measure this?
  3. How should it be collected?
  4. Who should you collect it from?
  5. When should you collect it?

Critically, these decisions must be made before the project starts — not after — as choices made too late can compromise the entire evaluation.

What Do You Need to Measure?

The main purpose of data collection is to test the assumptions embedded in the Theory of Change — which means gathering information at every step where the causal logic could break down. Beyond outcomes, it is important to capture covariates: background characteristics such as gender, location, or firm size that may explain why an intervention works differently for different groups. Decisions about how to account for these differences, through stratification for instance, should be made upfront. Compliance also needs to be tracked — understanding who actually took up the intervention, and why, is essential for interpreting results. Finally, the timing of data collection must reflect the different purposes it serves: baseline data, take-up information, and both intermediate and final outcome measures all require separate planning before the intervention begins.

What Type of Data Is Needed?

There are two broad types of data available to evaluators. Primary data is information collected directly from target respondents — such as individuals, research organizations, or firms — either by the organization itself or through contracted intermediaries. Secondary data, by contrast, draws on existing sources, particularly administrative records and data gathered by other organizations or government agencies. Both have distinct strengths and limitations, and the right choice depends on the specific needs of the evaluation.

How Should Data Be Collected?

When collecting primary data, the method should be chosen based on what works best for the respondents and the context — whether that is an in-person survey, a phone interview, or an online questionnaire. Digital collection tools are preferable where possible, as they reduce errors and speed up processing. The instrument should always be piloted before full deployment, and quality controls should be built in throughout the process.

For secondary and administrative data, the key is to identify the data owner as early as possible, since access negotiations and data-sharing agreements can take considerable time. Before committing to a source, its quality should be carefully assessed — checking for completeness, consistency, and clear variable definitions. GDPR compliance must also be confirmed before any data is transferred.

Regardless of the data type, collection choices should be matched to the indicators and the Theory of Change. It is also worth noting that primary and secondary data can be combined — for example, using administrative records as a baseline and primary survey data for follow-up. Before investing in new data collection, teams should thoroughly assess what data already exists and whether it can serve the evaluation’s needs at lower cost.

Who Should You Collect Data From?

For primary data, the goal is to reach respondents who are knowledgeable about the outcome of interest and have little reason to misreport. The choice of respondent should also match the unit of analysis: for firm-level interventions, for instance, a senior decision-maker such as a C-suite executive is typically the most appropriate source. It is also worth thinking carefully about who within an organisation actually benefits from the intervention — the firm as a whole, or a specific group such as R&D staff — as this shapes who should be surveyed.

For secondary and administrative data, the starting point is identifying which organisation or agency holds the most relevant records — whether that is a business registry, tax authority, customs office, or patent office. Where multiple sources are available, linking them through a common identifier such as a VAT number can produce a much richer picture.

In both cases, the unit of data collection should align with the unit of randomization, and data should be collected from the same respondent or source at both baseline and endline to ensure results are comparable over time.

When Should You Collect Data?

For primary data, baseline information must be collected before randomization and before the intervention begins. The timing of the endline should reflect when effects can realistically be expected to have materialised — collecting too early risks missing impacts that take time to emerge. Depending on the intervention, intermediate rounds of data collection may also be needed to track progress along the Theory of Change.

For secondary and administrative data, pre-intervention records should be requested as early as possible to establish baseline trends. The frequency and timing of data transfers should be agreed with the data owner in advance, and reporting lags must be factored in — administrative data is often published with a delay. It is also important to be aware of the release calendar of administrative sources, as availability may be annual, quarterly, or irregular, which directly affects when meaningful analysis can be conducted.

Across both data types, survey fatigue should be avoided by spacing out collection rounds and keeping each one as concise as possible. Teams should also be mindful of seasonality, steering clear of periods that could distort results such as fiscal year-ends or holiday periods. Finally, the timing of every data collection round should be documented to ensure transparency and reproducibility.
