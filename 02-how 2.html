How

Randomisation must be designed in from the start

An RCT is not something that can be added to a programme after launch. The comparison group, the eligibility rules, and the baseline data all have to be settled before the first grant is awarded.

In SMART Romania, one survey did three jobs: application, eligibility check, and baseline data collection, all in a single step. In October 2023, 519 of the eligible firms were then randomly selected for funding, and 519 were not.

Choosing the group size

To detect the impact of a programme, you need enough firms in both the treatment and comparison groups to reliably tell a real effect apart from the ordinary differences between firms. Statisticians call this statistical power: with too few firms, even a programme that works can appear not to. The smaller the effect you want to detect, the more firms you need. Rules of thumb and power calculations are covered in Advanced How.

Two ways to increase your sample

Loosen the eligibility bar

If fewer firms qualify than you’d like, lowering the eligibility threshold lets more firms into the pool you randomise from.

Widen the ranking bins

If you’re ranking firms by a score, don’t treat the ranking as exact. Group similarly-scored firms into bands, and treat everyone in a band as equally eligible, so more firms end up in the randomised pool rather than being decided by their exact rank.

A bigger pool also gives you room to test more than one version of the programme at once – more on that under Multiple treatment arms, below.

Five practical ways to randomise your programme

Not every programme fits the same shape. Here are five common ways to randomise one.

Lottery

How: randomly select who gets the programme, by public or private lottery.

Best when: eligibility is clear and the programme is oversubscribed.

Multiple treatment arms

How: randomly assign different versions of the programme, with or without a control group.

Best when: you want to know which design works best, not just whether it works.

Randomised phase-in

How: everyone gets the programme eventually, in a random order. Those still waiting are the comparison group.

Best when: budget or capacity cannot cover everyone at once.

Randomised intensities

How: vary how much of the programme a firm gets, for example 100%, 50%, or 0% of eligible employees.

Best when: spillovers within or between firms are a concern.

Encouragement design

How: randomise who gets extra outreach to take up the programme, since it cannot be denied to anyone.

Best when: take-up is voluntary and the programme itself cannot be withheld.

SMART Romania used a lottery

Of the five designs above, SMART Romania used a lottery: 1 038 eligible firms were randomly split into a treatment group of 519, invited to apply for a grant, and a comparison group of 519, not invited.

Running a lottery: transparency vs. behavioural responses

Public lottery

A public draw, for example pulling names from a hat.

Advantage: transparent and fair.

Disadvantage: the comparison group may change its behaviour once it knows the result.

Private lottery

A lottery run behind the scenes.

Advantage: avoids the comparison group reacting to a public result.

Disadvantage: less visibly fair.

In practice, many World Bank-supported evaluations run the draw as a secure, documented, computerised procedure that can be re-checked afterwards.

Either kind of lottery can also be narrowed to just part of the pool: rather than randomising everyone who’s eligible, you can randomise only the firms near the eligibility cutoff, and decide the clear-cut cases directly.

Narrowing the pool: a lottery around a cutoff

Score firms on eligibility criteria. Accept the top scorers outright, reject the bottom outright, and randomise only the firms in between.

Best when: eligibility criteria are clear and the programme is oversubscribed, with limited resources.

Keep selection rules-based. Subjective rankings, even by judges or expert panels, have been shown to introduce hidden bias that a lottery or scored cutoff avoids.

Rule-based scoring beats judgment-based scoring

Across several studies, subjective judgment struggles to identify future winners.

Judges favour their own group

A study of court rulings in Kenya found that defendants were more likely to win if they shared the judge’s ethnicity or gender.

Chen, Daniel L., et al., “Do Judges Favour Their Own Ethnicity and Gender?” (2022).

Judges do not beat a simple survey

In a business plan competition, a short applicant survey predicted future firm growth slightly better than expert judge panels did on their own.

Fafchamps, Marcel, and Christopher Woodruff, “Identifying gazelles: Expert panels vs. surveys,” World Bank Economic Review 31(3) (2017).

Judges, and algorithms, both struggle

Comparing judges’ scores, researcher prediction models, and machine learning in a Nigerian business competition, none of the three approaches predicted which firms actually survived, grew, or turned a profit three years later.

McKenzie, David, and Dario Sansone, “Predicting entrepreneurial success is hard,” Journal of Development Economics 141 (2019).

Subjective judgment, even from experts, struggles to reliably pick future winners. This is part of why rules-based methods – lotteries or scored cutoffs – are often the fairer and more defensible way to allocate a programme, rather than discretionary picks.

Multiple treatment arms: testing programme versions

Randomly assign firms (or CEOs, employees) to different versions of the programme, and compare them against each other and against the comparison group. This also works without a comparison group at all, if you only want to compare versions.

Best when: you’re uncertain which component or design actually drives results, and want to know which version works best, not just whether the programme works.

Advantage: helps investigate the channels of impact. Trade-off: needs a larger sample size, since it’s split across more groups.

Randomised phase-in: everyone gets it, just not at once

Everybody eventually receives the programme, just at different, randomly assigned times. Whoever is scheduled last serves as the comparison group in the meantime. Leave enough time between waves for impacts to develop.

Best when: budget, time, or capacity constraints mean you can’t roll the programme out to everyone at once.

Advantages: everyone eventually receives the programme, which makes the decision transparent and easier to justify; it also lets you study how impact changes with different treatment durations. Trade-off: you can’t observe long-term impacts, since the comparison group eventually receives the programme too.

Randomised intensities: varying how much firms get

Randomly vary how much of the programme each firm gets: for example, some firms get it for 100% of eligible employees, others for only part, e.g. 50%, and others none at all (the comparison group).

Best when: spillovers are likely – for example, information spreading between treated and untreated employees within the same firm.

Advantage: lets you analyse possible spillovers, not just the direct effect. Trade-off: needs a bigger sample size to detect effects at each intensity level.

Facing low take-up? Randomise the encouragement

The problem: low take-up. The firms who need a programme most can be the least likely to take it up – badly managed firms often don’t know they’re badly managed. Low take-up also makes it hard to tell whether a programme is working.

Solution: encouragement design. When you can’t deny the programme to anyone – for example, an open voucher scheme – randomise who gets extra outreach or information encouraging them to take it up, then compare the encouraged group with the not-encouraged group.

Advantage: works even when everyone must have access to the programme. Trade-off: only captures part of the impact, and can be biased if untargeted encouragement – TV or radio, for example – reaches the comparison group too.

Compliance is rarely perfect: some encouraged firms never take up the programme, and some not-encouraged firms take it up anyway. Compare the whole encouraged group with the whole not-encouraged group regardless.

Still not convinced? More arguments for randomising

Free and fair competition

The researcher determines allocation, not personal connections or favouritism, so there’s no selection bias – no systematic difference between groups on average. It can be designed to answer precisely the question you need answered, especially when the effect of a policy is uncertain.

Equality and non-discrimination

Often the outcome of a policy isn’t clear, and it could even be negative. Testing first, rather than rolling it out to everyone, protects people from that uncertainty. You can also make sure everyone still receives the core programme, and only randomise an add-on whose effect isn’t yet known.

Publicity and transparency

A randomised design needs fewer additional assumptions than other evaluation methods. Because the analysis is less complex, it’s easier to explain and defend the findings to the people affected by them.
